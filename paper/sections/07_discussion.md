# Discussion {#sec:discussion}

**What to do at upgrade time.** Our results suggest three inexpensive habits for a team about to switch an agent to the next model in its family. First, never act on a single failed run: re-run each failure up to twice and flag only tasks that fail every time. In our data this removes all false alarms from one-shot comparisons for about a fifth more episodes, and the cleverer first-action probing we tried does not do better. Second, check the environment per episode, not per job, and refuse to grade any episode in which a tool returned a connection error; our own per-job check passed while the server was down for ten hours. Third, audit unlicensed writes separately from success. The upgrades we studied mostly reduced them, but this is a property of the pairs we studied rather than a law, and a reduction in overreach is invisible in a success rate that already penalises it.

**Why the "broken in places" story did not survive.** We began this study expecting, from our pilot and from work on single-turn benchmarks [@cacioli2026beyond; @sheng2026nouniversal], that same-family upgrades would be better on average but reliably break specific workflows. On clean data they are better on average, often by a wide margin on the operations testbed, and the reliable breaks we see are few and individually indistinguishable from what three runs of the same model produce. Two caveats keep this from being an all-clear. A handful of cells have more reliable negative flips than chance before correction for multiple testing (for example, two open-weight pairs on the clinical testbed), and a team that depends on one of those tasks experiences a real regression whatever the corrected p-value says. And with three runs per model, a reliable flip is a coarse event; a regression that lowers a task's success rate from 90% to 40% will usually not appear as one. Teams with a small set of critical workflows should therefore run those workflows many more times than three, which is cheap when the set is small.

**Pre-registration and its limits.** Pre-registration did its job twice here. It forced us to report five unmet hypotheses, one of them (H1) missing its criterion only at the boundary, and its exclusion rule, written before any data existed, told us what to do when we found the outage. It did not prevent us from forming a new hypothesis (H6, reliability erosion) on data we did not yet know was corrupted. The confirmatory test of that hypothesis on the two uncorrupted testbeds failed, as did its exploratory version on the repaired clinical data. We report it as a cautionary example: an amendment is only as sound as the data that motivated it.

# Conclusion {#sec:conclusion}

We audited six same-family model upgrades of tool-using agents on three executable testbeds, three runs per model per task, under a pre-registered protocol. At least half of the regressions a one-shot comparison reports are noise, and re-running failures removes them cheaply. Reliable regressions are rare, and newer generations write without licence less often. The largest effect in our study came not from any model but from a silent environment outage, which, before we caught it, had turned three upgrade gains into apparent losses and produced a "reliable" break in a provider's official replacement model. For agent evaluation, checking the environment on every episode matters as much as running each episode more than once.
